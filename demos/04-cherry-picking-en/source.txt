In the panel debate on whether structured programming instruction should be
introduced as a mandatory subject in primary school, the moderator opened by
listing four findings that the panel had agreed on in advance.

First, in three pilot districts that introduced a weekly one-hour coding block
for ages 8 to 10, teachers reported a measurable lift in students' willingness
to attempt multi-step problems in unrelated subjects. The effect size was small
but consistent across all three districts.

Second, the same pilot districts reported that students from households without
a home computer fell further behind their peers on the in-class coding tasks
within the first semester. The gap did not close by the end of the school year,
and in one district it widened. The panel labeled this an equity warning that
must be addressed before any wider rollout.

Third, teacher preparation was identified as the dominant cost driver. Two of
the three districts reported that without at least 40 hours of subject-specific
teacher training, the coding block degraded into unsupervised screen time and
produced no measurable benefit. One district that under-invested in training
saw a net negative effect on math scores.

Fourth, the panel was explicit that none of the pilot data supported a claim
that early coding instruction improves long-term academic outcomes. The
follow-up window was only one school year, and the panel cautioned against
extrapolating from short-term enthusiasm to long-term skill formation.

The moderator closed by stating that the panel's consensus was conditional
support, not endorsement: a primary-school coding block can be defended only
when the equity gap, the teacher-training cost, and the modest evidence base
are all addressed in the rollout plan.
