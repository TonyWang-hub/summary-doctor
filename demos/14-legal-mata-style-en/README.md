# Demo 14 — Legal sanctions order summarization (English)

This demo is synthetic. The source and summary are entirely invented for
the purpose of illustrating one failure shape — AI summaries of a court
order that fabricate a case citation and reverse the court's finding.
The fictional citation "Lee v. Acme Corp, 2024 WL 99999" is
obvious-by-design; any resemblance to real parties, attorneys, judges,
firms, or dockets is unintended.

**This demo is a stylized reproduction of a *failure pattern* documented
in real public sanctions opinions (see "real events" below). It does not
reproduce, quote, or paraphrase any specific real sanctions order.**

## Why this failure shape matters in legal practice

A US federal court entered a sanctions order in 2023 against attorneys
who filed a brief containing case citations generated by a public AI
chat tool. That order, and the body of follow-on jurisprudence that has
accumulated since, establish three baselines: (a) AI-fabricated case
citations are reaching judicial proceedings, (b) attorneys have an
independent duty to verify authority regardless of the drafting tool,
and (c) the regulatory tailwind is a *professional obligation*, not just
a recommendation. The real public events listed below establish those
baselines; this synthetic demo is one stylized instance of the failure
shape they document.

| Real public event | What happened | Source URL |
|---|---|---|
| *Mata v. Avianca, Inc.*, S.D.N.Y., June 22, 2023 (Rule 11 sanctions order) | A federal district court imposed a five-thousand-dollar sanction on two attorneys and their firm after they filed a brief containing six citations to AI-generated cases that did not exist. The court ordered notice to each falsely-named judge. | <https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/new-york/nysdce/1:2022cv01461/575368/54/> |
| American Bar Association Formal Opinion 512, July 29, 2024 | The ABA's first formal ethics opinion on generative AI required attorneys to "understand the capacity and limitations of GAI" and to ensure "accuracy of all genAI outputs" before submission to a tribunal. | <https://www.americanbar.org/news/abanews/aba-news-archives/2024/07/aba-issues-first-ethics-guidance-ai-tools/> |
| Stanford RegLab — hallucination rate study, May 2024 | A Stanford RegLab benchmark of two leading commercial legal-AI tools found hallucination rates of 17 percent and 17–34 percent on legal queries, despite vendor marketing claims of "hallucination-free" output. | <https://reglab.stanford.edu/publications/hallucination-free-assessing-the-reliability-of-leading-ai-legal-research-tools/> |

The pattern across the three events: AI-written legal summaries can
fabricate authority and misstate court findings, and the duty to detect
that has been formally placed on the human attorney.

## Expected audit

The summary in this demo carries two distinct failure shapes mixed in
with two control claims that the source actually supports.

| Claim from summary | Expected label | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "the court relied centrally on Lee v. Acme Corp, 2024 WL 99999" | **fabricated** | No case by that name, citation, or reporter appears anywhere in the source. The citation is invented and is the load-bearing authority for the summary's incorrect framing of the court's reasoning. |
| "the court exonerated the two attorneys of record and their firm, finding that the attorneys had not violated any duty of candor" | **reversed** | The source says the opposite: the court *sanctioned* the attorneys and their firm, imposed a monetary penalty, ordered notice to each judge falsely named, and required additional professional-responsibility training. The summary inverts the disposition. |
| "the order described the case as turning on whether the attorneys had generated the citations using a public AI chat tool and had failed to verify the cited cases" | exact | Source carries the same framing of the issue. |
| "the sanction was not entered because of the use of an AI tool in itself, but because the attorneys abandoned the verification step that the rules of professional conduct require regardless of the drafting medium" | exact | Source carries this sentence almost verbatim. |

`reversed` and `fabricated` are the high-signal labels — at least one of
each must appear for this demo to be considered "live".

## Why this is hard

The reversed disposition ("exonerated" vs the source's "sanctioned") is
a one-word polarity flip in a sentence whose surrounding boilerplate
("sanctions proceeding", "court", "attorneys of record") matches the
source closely. A reader who skims the surrounding clauses will miss
that the actual disposition has been inverted. The fabricated citation
is the more dangerous failure shape because it is the load-bearing
authority for the (incorrect) legal reasoning the summary attributes to
the court — a downstream lawyer relying on this summary as the basis for
a brief would import an invented holding from an invented case into a
real filing, which is exactly the failure pattern documented in the real
public events above.

## How to run

```bash
summary-doctor demos/14-legal-mata-style-en/summary.txt \
  --original demos/14-legal-mata-style-en/source.txt \
  --lang en --mock
```

`--mock` is heuristic. Because the fabricated citation shares surface
tokens with the source ("court", "AI", "verification"), the mock backend
will likely flag it as `reversed` or `softened` rather than as
`fabricated`. The Anthropic backend (`--mock` removed,
`ANTHROPIC_API_KEY` exported) is the calibrated path and is expected to
recover the citation-fabrication label.
