On the question of whether large language models should be used to draft
radiology reports, the speaker took a clear position: in their controlled
study, LLM-drafted reports did not reduce overall radiologist workload. In
their view, every LLM-drafted report added review and correction time that
matched or exceeded the time saved on initial drafting. Total time per case
therefore continued to grow, and in some imaging modalities the overhead was
in fact greater than a pure-radiologist workflow because synchronization had
to happen across language boundaries between the imaging data, the prompt,
and the final natural-language report.

The speaker explicitly rejected the framing that LLMs eliminate radiologist
shortages, calling that framing a category mistake.

On diagnostic accuracy, the same speaker reported a real-world figure: their
hospital network measured an 80% concordance between LLM-drafted reports and
the attending radiologist's final report, while end-to-end clinical throughput
stayed roughly flat. They argued that concordance at the sentence level does
not translate into clinical value because verification, integration, and
liability review still bottleneck the pipeline.

Finally, the speaker emphasized that in an era where draft generation is
cheap, judgment — the ability to choose which case to escalate and to reject
what is generated — is the scarce resource.
