In a public lecture on the environmental footprint of large-scale
scientific computing, the speaker — a senior researcher in numerical
climate modeling — laid out three findings from a multi-year audit of a
mid-sized academic computing cluster.

First, the audit found that water consumption attributable to cooling
infrastructure had been systematically under-reported in prior published
inventories. The corrected figure, after accounting for evaporative losses
in the cluster's adiabatic cooling stage, was roughly 2.4 times the
previously reported number. The speaker stressed that this correction
applies only to the specific cluster studied; it should not be
extrapolated to facilities that use closed-loop liquid cooling.

Second, the audit measured embodied carbon — that is, the carbon emitted
in the manufacture of the server hardware itself — against operational
carbon over a six-year service life. The two were of comparable magnitude,
with embodied carbon accounting for between 38 and 46 percent of
lifetime emissions depending on which regional electricity grid the
cluster was connected to. The speaker explicitly rejected the popular
framing that "running compute is dirty and building hardware is clean";
both contribute, and a cluster sited on a carbon-intensive grid will see
operational emissions dominate, while one sited on a clean grid will see
embodied carbon dominate.

Third, the audit examined the effect of workload scheduling on emissions
and found that delay-tolerant batch workloads, when shifted in time to
align with periods of higher renewable supply on the grid, reduced
operational carbon by 9 to 14 percent. The speaker cautioned that the
14 percent upper bound was observed only on one specific regional grid
during one specific season and that the figure should not be quoted as
a generic expected outcome.

The speaker concluded that the most impactful single intervention for the
specific cluster audited was the cooling-water correction itself, because
correcting the inventory is a prerequisite for any subsequent reduction
target to be measurable. The speaker did not claim that cooling-water
correction is the most impactful intervention for scientific computing in
general; on the contrary, she said that the ranking of interventions is
strongly cluster-specific.
